# My Banker Serbia — call notes: ten hard questions

This is a companion for the Serbia discussion. It is not an attack document. Its purpose is to turn an approved idea into a product that can be designed, explained, and defended to a client.

## Opening position

> To design this experience correctly, I need to understand what we promise the client, why a product appears first, and what makes it relevant. Otherwise I can design a catalogue, but I cannot honestly design a personal recommendation.

Ask these questions as product inputs needed for copy, hierarchy, ranking, and exclusion cases — not as an audit.

---

## 1. Group ownership is not individual need

**Question**

> You measure the probability that a group holds a product. How do you turn that into the probability that this client needs the product or that the product is suitable for them?

**What it tests**

The model has `product held by peers`, while the screen claims `product suitable for this client`. Those are different claims.

**Likely answer**

“It is only a propensity signal, not certainty.”

**Follow-up**

> Great. Propensity towards what exactly: an application, approval, profitability, healthy repayment, or client benefit? Where is the validation that peer ownership predicts that outcome?

**Your note**

Do not request data as an auditor. Say: “I need this to know what we can honestly say on screen.”

---

## 2. Percentages are not choices between products

**Question**

> When 63% of peers have an overdraft and 54% have a credit card, how many are the same people? If many hold both, what does the ranking tell us about a choice between them?

**What it tests**

Ownership percentages are not a preference ranking. The same people can hold several products; the higher percentage was not necessarily chosen instead of the lower one.

**Likely answer**

“We do not claim the products are exclusive; we only show adoption.”

**Follow-up**

> Then why do we use adoption to set the order between products? If it does not represent a choice between them, it cannot justify the ranking.

---

## 3. How much adoption is actually client choice?

**Question**

> How much of the product’s adoption comes from a client’s free choice, and how much from historic campaigns, pre-approvals, automatic inclusion, availability, or RM activity?

**What it tests**

The percentage can reflect the bank’s historical distribution strategy, not client preference or client benefit.

**Likely answer**

“Clients still hold the products, so they remain relevant.”

**Follow-up**

> Ownership tells us the product is open. What signal tells us it is wanted, used, and useful rather than simply still in the portfolio?

---

## 4. Where is the incremental sale?

**Question**

> How do we demonstrate that an application from this screen is new, rather than a client who would have applied through another channel or later anyway?

**What it tests**

More applications on this screen do not automatically prove incremental sales. The feature may simply move the channel or the timing.

**Likely answer**

“We will track views, simulations, and requests.”

**Follow-up**

> Those measure activity in the funnel. What comparison shows the effect against the situation where this screen did not exist?

**Your note**

This is one of the strongest questions for a team focused entirely on sales.

---

## 6. Twenty fixed lists are not individual analysis

**Question**

> We have five age bands and four income bands: twenty fixed lists. What individual signal changes one client’s list compared with another client in the same box?

**What it tests**

This is arithmetic: twenty segments, not an individual recommendation.

**Likely answer**

“Segmentation is a form of personalisation.”

**Follow-up**

> Yes. Let us name it accurately: this is segment personalisation. What justifies “recommended for you” for two people with very different financial situations in the same segment?

---

## 7. The rare but suitable product

**Question**

> If a product is rare in a peer group but exactly right for one client, what mechanism can lift it above the group’s bestseller?

**What it tests**

Sorting by adoption cannot elevate an individual match that contradicts group popularity.

**Likely answer**

“The client can scroll and find it.”

**Follow-up**

> Then the ranking is not a recommendation; it is a popularity list. Why do we give the popular product the primary attention?

---

## 8. Why is 63% positive while 12% is not negative?

**Question**

> If 12% is not an argument that a product is unsuitable, why is 63% an argument that it is suitable?

**What it tests**

The percentage has no stable meaning as a recommendation rule. If a low percentage does not determine suitability, a high percentage cannot prove it.

**Likely answer**

“We are interested in showing products with high percentages.”

**Follow-up**

> What do we do with a product the business wants to sell but only 12% of peers hold: hide it, show it with a number that weakens it, or ignore the percentage? In all three cases the percentage is no longer neutral analysis.

---

## 9. What actually triggers credit demand?

**Question**

> Take the last 50 credits issued in this segment. What triggered each application, and how many were triggered by peer comparison?

**What it tests**

Credit is usually triggered by an event or need: a car, renovation, expense, refinancing, or liquidity. Peer percentage is not that moment.

**Likely answer**

“The feature can create new interest.”

**Follow-up**

> If the person already had a need, how does the percentage help them make a better choice? If they had no need, what concrete client benefit does the new interest create?

---

## 10. The self-validating loop

**Question**

> How do we separate a product’s genuine popularity from the popularity created by our own screen after we place it first?

**What it tests**

A product appears first because it is popular. More people see and take it. It becomes even more popular. The model can confuse the effect of its own campaign with independent client preference.

**Likely answer**

“That is good; we want adoption to grow.”

**Follow-up**

> Then the percentage is no longer independent evidence that the product is relevant. It is an effect of our campaign. Why do we present it to the client as an independent argument?

---

## If they rename it “Our Products”

**Do not let the discussion end with a naming change.**

> Fine. If this is “Our Products”, then it is a catalogue. What job do Similar Clients, Age, Monthly Income, peer percentages, and peer ranking perform in a catalogue?

They have only two answers:

1. **“The peer layer does not change a decision; it is only information.”**
   - Then remove it. A client does not need demographic peer analysis to browse the bank’s products.

2. **“The peer layer makes the catalogue more persuasive or more personalised.”**
   - Then it remains a sales mechanism regardless of the title. Ask:
   > What measurable uplift does this peer layer create over exactly the same catalogue without it?

**Closing line**

> Changing “Recommended products” to “Our Products” removes only one claim. It does not explain why the peer-analysis mechanism exists.

---

## Closing statement

> I am not asking us to abandon Serbia’s ambition. I am asking us not to turn an ownership percentage into a personal recommendation before we define what is recommended, for whom, why, and how we know it works. We can retain Core, the simulator, product data, and the RM request. The problem is the peer-ranking rule for credit.
